# AI Ethics Home

## Purpose

This knowledge base provides a structured approach to studying the ethical, social, technical, and governance dimensions of artificial intelligence.

It is designed not merely as a glossary, but as a connected knowledge and reasoning structure linking:

**values & rights → ethical principles → harms & risks → requirements → controls → evidence → decisions → monitoring**

Its purpose is to support conceptual learning, critical reasoning, and systematic analysis of real-world AI systems and the socio-technical contexts in which they operate.

> The structure of this knowledge base is an evolving organizational framework rather than a claim that AI Ethics has a single universally accepted taxonomy.

## Core Ethical Questions

When evaluating an AI system, ask:

1. **Purpose & Alternatives** — What is the system intended to achieve, should AI be used for this task, and are less intrusive, less risky, or non-AI alternatives available?

2. **Stakeholders & Power** — Who benefits, who is affected, who bears the risks, and who has decision-making power?

3. **Rights & Justice** — Which rights, freedoms, opportunities, or forms of disadvantage may be affected?

4. **Data** — What data are used, how were they generated, what may be missing, and whose interests or circumstances may be represented or overlooked?

5. **Bias & Fairness** — Where can bias or unequal treatment arise, and which conception of fairness is ethically relevant?

6. **Transparency & Explanation** — What should different stakeholders be able to know, understand, or question about the system and its decisions?

7. **Privacy & Data Governance** — Are personal and sensitive data collected, inferred, shared, retained, and governed appropriately?

8. **Human Agency** — Where must humans retain meaningful authority, judgment, or control?

9. **Safety, Security & Reliability** — How can the system fail, be manipulated, degrade, or behave outside its intended conditions?

10. **Accountability** — Who is answerable for the system, its decisions, and its consequences?

11. **Contestability & Redress** — Can affected people challenge a decision, obtain meaningful review, and seek correction or remedy?

12. **Governance & Legitimacy** — Which institutional controls, standards, regulations, and oversight mechanisms apply, who has decision-making authority, and is the system's use institutionally legitimate?

13. **Evidence & Uncertainty** — What evidence supports claims about performance, fairness, safety, reliability, and other relevant properties, and what remains uncertain?

14. **Misuse & Manipulation** — How could the system be intentionally misused, exploited, or used to manipulate people or information environments?

15. **Sustainability** — What environmental, resource, and broader sustainability impacts arise across the system's lifecycle?

16. **Lifecycle & Monitoring** — How will risks, impacts, assumptions, and system performance be monitored as the system and its environment change?

17. **Intervention & Stop Criteria** — What evidence would justify investigation, escalation, restriction, suspension, redesign, rollback, or retirement of the system?

## From Principles to Practice

A central reasoning chain in this knowledge base is:

**Human Value / Right**

↓

**Ethical Principle**

↓

**Ethical Requirement**

↓

**Operational Criterion**

↓

**Metric / Evaluation Method / Evidence Requirement**

↓

**Acceptance Criterion / Threshold / Decision Rule**

↓

**Technical or Organizational Control**

↓

**Verification / Assurance Evidence**

↓

**Residual Risk**

↓

**Governance Decision**

↓

**Deployment Conditions**

↓

**Monitoring Indicator**

↓

**Trigger / Escalation / Stop Criterion**

↓

**Reassessment**

This chain illustrates how ethical commitments can be translated into operational requirements, controls, evidence, governance decisions, and lifecycle management without reducing ethics to metrics alone.

It also preserves two important distinctions:

> **Evidence ≠ Decision**

and:

> **Risk estimation ≠ Risk acceptability**

## Cross-Cutting Ethical Tensions & Potential Trade-offs

Ethical evaluation of AI rarely reduces to maximizing a single desirable property.

Potential tensions may arise between objectives such as:

- Fairness vs Performance
- Privacy vs Fairness Auditing
- Accuracy vs Explainability
- Transparency vs Security
- Safety vs Autonomy
- Personalization vs Privacy
- Innovation vs Precaution

These relationships should not automatically be assumed to represent unavoidable trade-offs.

Whether a genuine conflict exists depends on:

- the system and decision context
- affected stakeholders
- relevant rights and values
- available technical and organizational alternatives
- the severity and distribution of potential harms
- reversibility of relevant consequences
- uncertainty in the available evidence

> **A trade-off should be demonstrated rather than assumed.**

## Knowledge Conventions

This knowledge base distinguishes among:

- **descriptive claims** — statements about what is observed, measured, or documented

- **empirical evidence** — observations or research findings that support, challenge, or qualify a claim

- **causal claims** — claims about mechanisms or causal relationships proposed to explain observed outcomes

- **interpretive claims** — reasoned interpretations connecting evidence to explanations, mechanisms, or broader conclusions

- **normative claims** — judgments about what ought to be done, required, permitted, prohibited, or considered acceptable

- **formal criteria** — mathematical or technical operationalizations of concepts or requirements

- **operational requirements** — conditions that a system, process, or organization is expected to satisfy

- **regulatory requirements** — obligations established by applicable law or regulation

- **standards and guidance** — structured specifications, recommendations, or practices issued by recognized bodies

- **contested interpretations** — concepts or conclusions for which legitimate disagreement remains

- **uncertainty** — limitations in evidence, measurement, inference, prediction, causal attribution, or generalizability

- **open questions** — issues requiring further research, deliberation, empirical investigation, or ethical reasoning

These categories should not be treated as interchangeable.

For example:

**Observed disparity**

↓

may support an empirical claim

but does not automatically establish:

**Causal explanation**

or:

**Ethical judgment**

or:

**Legal discrimination**

Each step may require additional evidence and justification.

> **No single metric, audit, assessment, framework, standard, regulation, or certification is sufficient on its own to establish the overall ethical acceptability of an AI system or its use.**
