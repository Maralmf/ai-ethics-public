# What is AI Ethics?

## Definition

AI Ethics is the interdisciplinary field concerned with the normative evaluation of how artificial intelligence systems are conceived, designed, developed, deployed, used, monitored, and governed.

It examines how AI systems affect human values, rights, interests, opportunities, institutions, social relationships, and distributions of power, and asks what principles, obligations, constraints, and forms of accountability ought to govern those effects.

AI Ethics therefore concerns not only algorithms or model outputs, but the wider socio-technical systems in which AI is embedded.

---

## Core Question

At its most fundamental level, AI Ethics asks:

> **What ought humans, organizations, and institutions do when developing, deploying, using, and governing AI systems?**

This question cannot be answered by technical performance alone.

An AI system may be:

- highly accurate but discriminatory
- explainable but harmful
- privacy-preserving but unjust
- robust but used for an illegitimate purpose
- legally compliant but ethically questionable
- human-supervised but lacking meaningful human control

Therefore:

> **Technical quality is relevant to ethical evaluation, but it is not sufficient to establish the ethical acceptability of an AI system or its use.**

---

## AI Ethics Is Normative, Interdisciplinary, and Evidence-Informed

AI Ethics is centrally **normative** because it asks:

- what ought to be done
- what should be permitted or prohibited
- what obligations exist
- which rights should be protected
- what risks may be legitimately imposed
- what forms of treatment can be ethically justified

However, normative reasoning does not operate independently of empirical evidence.

AI Ethics draws on disciplines including:

- philosophy and applied ethics
- computer science and machine learning
- law and human rights
- social and political science
- human-computer interaction
- psychology and human factors
- safety and security engineering
- organizational studies
- public policy and governance

A useful distinction is:

**Empirical inquiry**

→ What is happening?

**Causal inquiry**

→ What mechanisms or causal relationships may explain what is happening?

**Normative inquiry**

→ Is it ethically acceptable, and what ought to be done?

These questions are related but not interchangeable.

## Why AI Raises Ethical Questions

AI systems can affect people, organizations, and institutions at substantial scale.

They may influence:

- employment
- credit and financial services
- healthcare
- education
- insurance
- policing and criminal justice
- public services
- surveillance
- workplace management
- information environments
- political participation
- access to social and economic opportunities

Several characteristics make ethical evaluation particularly important.

### Scale

Automated systems may affect thousands or millions of people.

A relatively small systematic problem can therefore produce substantial aggregate or cumulative harm.

---

### Automation and Delegation

AI systems can automate or mediate parts of human judgment and organizational decision-making.

This raises questions concerning:

- authority
- responsibility
- accountability
- human oversight
- contestability
- appropriate reliance

---

### Data Dependence

AI systems learn from or operate on data that may reflect:

- historical inequalities
- institutional practices
- measurement limitations
- incomplete representation
- labeling decisions
- social bias

Related concepts:

- Historical Bias
- Representation Bias
- Measurement Bias
- Label Bias

---

### Opacity and Complexity

Some AI systems may be difficult for users, operators, affected individuals, auditors, or even developers to understand.

Related concepts:

- Transparency
- Interpretability
- Explainability
- Black Box Problem

---

### Power

AI systems operate within relationships of institutional, informational, and decision-making power.

Relevant asymmetries may exist between:

- individuals and organizations
- citizens and governments
- employees and employers
- consumers and platforms
- patients and healthcare institutions

Related concepts:

- Power Asymmetry
- Information Asymmetry

---

### Adaptation and Change

An AI system that appears acceptable at deployment may become problematic over time as:

- populations change
- data distributions change
- institutional practices change
- users adapt their behavior
- models are updated
- deployment scope expands
- systems are repurposed

Related concepts:

- Post-Deployment Monitoring
- Data Drift
- Concept Drift
- Fairness Drift

Therefore:

> **Ethical acceptability is not necessarily permanent simply because a system passed an initial evaluation.**

---

## AI as a Socio-Technical System

A central principle of this knowledge base is that ethical outcomes do not arise from algorithms alone.

An AI-enabled decision may emerge from interactions among:

- problem formulation
- institutional objectives
- data collection
- sampling and representation
- measurement
- labels
- feature selection
- model design
- optimization objectives
- thresholds
- decision policies
- human operators
- organizational procedures
- institutional incentives
- applicable laws and standards
- deployment environments
- affected communities

Therefore:

> **An ethical problem observed in an AI-enabled decision should not automatically be attributed to the algorithm itself.**

A simplified chain is:

**Historical & Institutional Context**

↓

**Data Generation**

↓

**Measurement & Labels**

↓

**Model**

↓

**Decision Policy**

↓

**Human Interpretation**

↓

**Organizational Action**

↓

**Social Consequences**

This is why AI Ethics should evaluate the **entire socio-technical decision system**, not only model performance.

## From Empirical Observation to Normative Judgment

Consider the statement:

> Group A has a false-positive rate of 8%, while Group B has a false-positive rate of 19%.

This is an empirical observation.

It does not, by itself, establish:

> The system is ethically unfair.

Normative evaluation may additionally require consideration of:

- the consequences of false positives
- the validity of the outcome variable
- historical disadvantage
- affected rights
- decision context
- available alternatives
- stakeholder interests
- statistical uncertainty
- relevant fairness principles

Therefore:

> **Empirical evidence can inform ethical judgment, but it does not replace normative reasoning.**

A useful reasoning sequence is:

**Observation**

↓

**Interpretation**

↓

**Causal Investigation**

↓

**Normative Principle**

↓

**Ethical Judgment**

↓

**Decision**

## No Single Ethical Theory Determines Every AI Decision

Different ethical theories may generate different judgments about the same AI system.

For example, a system could:

- maximize aggregate welfare
- improve overall efficiency

while simultaneously:

- burdening a vulnerable minority
- infringing an important right
- reducing meaningful autonomy

AI Ethics therefore does not provide a universal formula of the form:

> ethical score ≥ threshold → ethical system

Instead, ethical evaluation requires reasoned justification across relevant:

- values
- rights
- harms
- evidence
- stakeholders
- alternatives
- institutional contexts

## AI Ethics Is Not the Same as Ethical AI

These terms should be distinguished.

### AI Ethics

AI Ethics is a field of normative inquiry.

It asks:

> **What ought we do regarding AI?**

### Ethical AI

Ethical AI refers to the aspiration that an AI system, its intended use, and the surrounding socio-technical arrangements are designed, deployed, and governed in ways that can be ethically justified.

It asks:

> **Can this system and its use be ethically justified?**

A useful working distinction is:

**AI Ethics**

→ ethical inquiry and reasoning

**Ethical AI**

→ aspiration toward ethically justifiable AI design, use, and governance

Important:

> **The phrase "Ethical AI" should not imply that the AI system itself is necessarily a moral agent.**

Responsibility generally remains distributed among humans, organizations, institutions, and governance structures involved throughout the AI lifecycle.

Related distinction:

- AI Ethics vs Ethical AI

## AI Ethics, Responsible AI, Trustworthy AI, and Ethical AI

These concepts overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

| Concept | Primary Question | Working Focus |
|---|---|---|
| **AI Ethics** | What ought we do regarding AI? | Normative inquiry and ethical justification |
| **Responsible AI** | How should responsibilities be operationalized? | Processes, requirements, controls, governance, and monitoring |
| **Trustworthy AI** | What properties, processes, and evidence justify appropriate reliance? | Trustworthiness, evidence, and assurance |
| **Ethical AI** | Can this system and its use be ethically justified? | Aspiration toward ethically justifiable design, use, and governance |

These terms are used differently across academic, policy, standards, and industry literature.

Therefore:

> **The distinctions above are working distinctions for this knowledge base, not claims that the terminology is universally standardized.**

## AI Ethics Is Not Equivalent to Compliance

Ethics and law overlap, but they are not identical.

A system may satisfy applicable legal requirements while still raising ethical concerns involving:

- fairness
- dignity
- autonomy
- manipulation
- power
- privacy
- social justice
- environmental impact

Conversely, ethical analysis may identify concerns not yet addressed by existing regulation.

Therefore:

> **Compliance with applicable law is legally required, but legal compliance alone is not sufficient to establish ethical acceptability.**

Related distinctions:

- Ethics vs Law
- Legal vs Ethical vs Legitimate
- Compliance vs Ethical Responsibility

## AI Ethics Is Not a Checklist

Ethical evaluation cannot generally be reduced to:

- one fairness metric
- one explainability method
- one risk score
- one impact assessment
- one audit
- one regulation
- one standard
- one principle
- one certification

Different objectives may interact or conflict.

Potential tensions include:

- Fairness vs Performance
- Privacy vs Fairness Auditing
- Accuracy vs Explainability
- Transparency vs Security
- Safety vs Autonomy
- Personalization vs Privacy
- Innovation vs Precaution

However:

> **A trade-off should be demonstrated rather than assumed.**

Some apparent conflicts may be reduced through:

- better problem formulation
- better data
- redesign
- different controls
- alternative workflows
- non-AI alternatives

Whether a genuine conflict exists depends on:

- the system and decision context
- affected stakeholders
- relevant rights and values
- available technical and organizational alternatives
- the severity and distribution of potential harms
- reversibility of relevant consequences
- uncertainty in the available evidence

## A Practical Ethical Evaluation Lens

A first-pass ethical assessment should ask:

### Purpose & Alternatives

What is the system intended to achieve?

Should AI be used for this task at all?

Could the objective be achieved through a less intrusive, less risky, or non-AI alternative?

---

### Stakeholders & Power

Who benefits?

Who bears the risks?

Who has decision-making power?

Who may have little realistic ability to refuse, avoid, or challenge the system?

---

### Rights & Justice

Which rights, freedoms, opportunities, or forms of disadvantage may be affected?

---

### Data

What data are used?

How were they generated?

Who is represented, missing, or poorly measured?

---

### Bias & Fairness

Where can bias or unequal treatment arise?

What conception of fairness is relevant?

---

### Transparency & Explanation

What should different stakeholders be able to know, understand, or question?

---

### Privacy & Data Governance

What personal or sensitive information is collected, inferred, retained, shared, or repurposed?

---

### Human Agency

Where must humans retain meaningful authority, judgment, or control?

---

### Safety, Security & Reliability

How can the system fail, degrade, be manipulated, or encounter conditions outside its assumptions?

---

### Accountability

Who is answerable for the system and its consequences?

---

### Contestability & Redress

Can affected individuals challenge a decision, obtain meaningful review, and seek correction or remedy?

---

### Governance & Legitimacy

Who has authority to approve, restrict, audit, or stop the system?

Which laws, standards, institutional controls, and oversight mechanisms apply?

Is the system's use institutionally legitimate?

---

### Evidence & Uncertainty

What evidence supports claims about:

- performance
- fairness
- safety
- reliability
- robustness
- other ethically relevant properties

What remains uncertain?

---

### Misuse & Manipulation

How could the system be deliberately misused, exploited, or used to manipulate people or information environments?

---

### Sustainability

What environmental, resource, or broader sustainability impacts arise across the system lifecycle?

---

### Lifecycle & Monitoring

How will risks, impacts, assumptions, and system behavior be monitored after deployment?

---

### Intervention & Stop Criteria

What evidence would justify:

- investigation
- escalation
- restriction
- suspension
- redesign
- rollback
- retirement

## Example — AI-Assisted Hiring

Consider an AI system used to recommend candidates for employment.

Suppose overall predictive performance is high, but among applicants labeled as qualified in the evaluation data, women receive positive recommendations less frequently than men.

High aggregate performance does not resolve the ethical question.

### Historical Context

Were previous hiring practices themselves discriminatory?

Related concept: Historical Bias

### Representation

Were relevant applicant populations adequately represented?

Related concept: Representation Bias

### Measurement

Do the variables adequately measure job-relevant constructs?

Related concept: Measurement Bias

### Labels

Does the designation "qualified" represent a defensible measure of job suitability?

Was a historical hiring, promotion, or performance decision treated as objective ground truth?

Related concept: Label Bias

### Fairness

If qualified applicants should have comparable opportunities to receive a positive recommendation, is an opportunity-based fairness criterion relevant?

Related concept: Equal Opportunity

### Explanation

Can an affected applicant obtain information that is meaningful for understanding or challenging the decision?

Related concept: Explainability

### Human Oversight

Can a human reviewer genuinely inspect, question, and override the recommendation?

Related concept: Meaningful Human Control

### Accountability

Who is answerable for the employment decision?

Related concept: Accountability

### Contestability

Can the applicant challenge the decision or underlying data?

Related concept: Contestability

### Redress

Can an incorrect or harmful decision be corrected or remedied?

Related concept: Redress

This example illustrates why AI Ethics evaluates the wider socio-technical decision process rather than any single model metric in isolation.

## Common Misconceptions

### "If the model is accurate, it is ethical."

False.

High performance does not establish fairness, legitimacy, privacy, safety, or ethical acceptability.

---

### "If protected attributes are removed, discrimination disappears."

False.

Related concepts:

- Proxy Bias
- Fairness Through Unawareness

---

### "If the model explains its decision, the decision is justified."

False.

> **Explanation ≠ Justification**

Related distinction:

- Explanation vs Justification

---

### "If a human is in the loop, humans remain meaningfully in control."

Not necessarily.

Related distinction:

- Human-in-the-Loop vs Meaningful Human Control

---

### "If the system complies with regulation, it is ethically acceptable."

Not necessarily.

Related distinction:

- Compliance vs Ethical Responsibility

---

### "If a fairness metric is satisfied, the system is fair."

Not necessarily.

Related concepts:

- Fairness Metric Conflicts
- Fairness Impossibility Results
- Statistical Fairness vs Ethical Fairness

## Critical Reasoning Principles

When analyzing an ethical claim about AI, ask:

1. What assumptions are being made?
2. What evidence supports the claim?
3. What evidence would falsify the conclusion?
4. What is the strongest counterargument?
5. What alternative explanation could produce the same result?
6. What methodological weaknesses could materially affect the conclusion?
7. Does the evidence support causation or only association?
8. What uncertainty remains?
9. Whose perspective, interests, or evidence may be missing?
10. What evidence or change in assumptions would make the conclusion or decision change?

Ethical reasoning therefore requires more than ethical principles.

It also requires critical analysis of:

- evidence
- assumptions
- causality
- uncertainty
- institutional context
- power
- alternative explanations

## Evidence Expectations

Claims in this knowledge base should be distinguished according to their evidentiary and normative status.

Relevant source categories include:

- peer-reviewed academic literature
- systematic and critical reviews
- books and scholarly reports
- international ethical and human-rights frameworks
- regulations and legal materials
- standards and technical guidance
- documented cases and incidents
- empirical evaluations

Sources differ in authority, purpose, evidentiary strength, methodological quality, relevance, and applicability.

Therefore:

> **No single paper, metric, audit, assessment, framework, standard, regulation, or certification should be treated as sufficient on its own to establish the overall ethical acceptability of an AI system or its use.**

Specific evidence should be maintained through dedicated literature, framework, case, and evidence notes.

## Open Questions

- Is there a universal minimum set of ethical requirements for AI?
- Which ethical principles should take priority when they conflict?
- Who has legitimate authority to define acceptable AI use?
- When should an apparently beneficial AI system nevertheless not be deployed?
- How should harms to individuals be considered alongside collective benefits?
- Can ethical acceptability be meaningfully assessed without affected stakeholder participation?
- How should uncertainty alter the burden of proof in high-risk deployment decisions?
- When should ethical concerns lead to redesign rather than mitigation?
- Which ethical requirements should operate as hard constraints rather than optimization objectives?
- What residual ethical risks, if any, may legitimately be accepted?
- Who has legitimate authority to accept residual risk on behalf of affected populations?
- What evidence should trigger restriction, suspension, redesign, or retirement?

## Core Takeaway

AI Ethics asks not merely:

> **Can this AI system work?**

but also:

> **Should it be used, under what conditions, for whose benefit, at whose risk, subject to which rights and obligations, supported by what evidence, and accountable to whom?**
