## Purpose

This learning path provides a structured introduction to AI Ethics for readers who do not require advanced technical knowledge.

Its purpose is to build a foundational understanding of how AI systems can affect people, rights, institutions, organizations, and society, and how ethical questions can be identified and reasoned about across the AI lifecycle.

The sequence is pedagogical rather than taxonomic: concepts are introduced in an order intended to support learning, not to imply that AI Ethics has a single universally accepted hierarchy.

---

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this learning path, you should be able to:

- explain why AI Ethics extends beyond technical performance
- distinguish AI Ethics, Ethical AI, Responsible AI, and Trustworthy AI at a basic conceptual level
- recognize AI systems as parts of wider socio-technical systems
- identify major stakeholders, harms, rights, vulnerabilities, and power asymmetries
- distinguish bias, disparity, fairness, and discrimination at an introductory level
- explain why statistical fairness does not by itself establish ethical fairness or justice
- distinguish transparency, interpretability, explainability, and ethical justification
- recognize the limits of nominal human oversight
- identify basic privacy, safety, security, accountability, and governance concerns
- recognize misuse, sustainability, and lifecycle concerns
- identify situations involving potential ethical trade-offs
- distinguish empirical observations from normative judgments
- ask structured ethical questions about a real AI system

### Key Question

> Why can a technically successful AI system still be ethically problematic?

### Core Insight

Technical performance is relevant to ethical evaluation, but it is not sufficient to establish the ethical acceptability of an AI system or its use.

AI systems should also be evaluated in relation to their purposes, users, institutions, affected stakeholders, decision processes, and social consequences.

### Key Distinction

> **AI Ethics ≠ Ethical AI ≠ Responsible AI ≠ Trustworthy AI**

These terms overlap, and their meanings vary across academic, policy, standards, and industry contexts.

In this knowledge base, they are used as working distinctions rather than as a universal hierarchy.

7. [Harm]
8. [Individual Harm]
9. [Group Harm]
10. [Vulnerability]
11. [Power Asymmetry]

### Key Question

> Who benefits from the system, who bears its risks, and who has the power to make, influence, refuse, or challenge decisions?

### Core Insight

AI systems can distribute benefits, burdens, opportunities, risks, and decision-making power unevenly across stakeholders.

Ethical analysis should therefore consider not only whether harm occurs, but also:

- who is exposed
- who is vulnerable
- who benefits
- who bears the consequences
- who determines whether the remaining risk is acceptable

# Stage 3 — Rights, Justice & Non-Discrimination

12. [Human Dignity]
13. [Human Rights]
14. [Justice]
15. [Non-Discrimination]

### Key Question

> Which rights, freedoms, opportunities, or forms of disadvantage may be affected by the system?

### Core Insight

Ethical evaluation involves more than measuring aggregate benefits or predictive performance.

It may also require examining:

- dignity
- rights
- equality
- justice
- discrimination
- access to opportunities
- distribution of benefits and burdens

A system can perform well statistically while still creating ethically significant disadvantages.

# Stage 4 — Bias & Fairness

16. [Historical Bias]
17. [Representation Bias]
18. [Algorithmic Bias]
19. [Fairness]
20. [Group Fairness]
21. [Bias vs Fairness]

### Key Question

> Can a model be accurate and still produce ethically problematic disparities?

### Core Insight

Bias and fairness are related but distinct.

**Bias** concerns mechanisms through which systematic distortion or disadvantage may arise.

**Fairness** concerns how treatment, procedures, outcomes, or distributions should be evaluated.

Therefore:

> **Disparity ≠ Bias ≠ Discrimination ≠ Injustice**

An observed disparity may warrant investigation without automatically establishing discrimination or injustice.

Conversely, satisfying a statistical fairness criterion does not by itself establish overall ethical fairness.

# Stage 5 — Transparency & Understanding

23. [Interpretability](Interpretability)
24. [Explainability](Explainability)
25. [Black Box Problem](<Black Box Problem>)
26. [Explanation vs Justification](<Explanation vs Justification>)

### Key Question

> Is explaining why an AI system produced a decision the same as justifying that decision?

### Core Insight

> **Explanation ≠ Justification**

Transparency, interpretability, and explainability can support scrutiny and understanding, but they do not establish that a decision is fair, legitimate, rights-respecting, or ethically justified.

A decision can be understandable while still being harmful or unacceptable.

# Stage 6 — Human Agency & Oversight

27. [Human Oversight]
28. [Human-in-the-Loop]
29. [Automation Bias]
30. [Meaningful Human Control]

### Key Question

> Does placing a human in the loop guarantee meaningful human control?

### Core Insight

> **Human presence ≠ Meaningful human control**

Humans may formally remain involved while lacking:

- sufficient information
- relevant competence
- decision-making authority
- adequate time
- practical ability to challenge the system
- organizational support for intervention

Human oversight should therefore be evaluated by its actual function rather than by the mere presence of a human reviewer.

# Stage 7 — Privacy & Data Responsibility

31. [Privacy](Privacy)
32. [Consent](Consent)
33. [Data Minimization]
34. [Purpose Limitation]

### Key Question

> What data should an AI system be allowed to collect, infer, retain, share, and use—and for what purpose?

### Core Insight

> **Data availability ≠ Ethical justification for data use**

Ethical data practices require consideration of:

- necessity
- proportionality
- consent
- purpose
- sensitivity
- retention
- inference
- secondary use
- affected rights

More data are not automatically better data.

# Stage 8 — Safety, Security & Reliability

35. [AI Safety]
36. [Reliability]
37. [Security]

### Key Question

> What happens when the system is wrong, manipulated, degraded, or used outside the conditions for which it was designed?

### Core Insight

Ethical evaluation should consider not only whether a system normally works, but also:

- how it can fail
- how severe failures may be
- who is exposed to failure
- whether failures can be detected
- whether the system can recover safely
- whether adversaries can exploit it

High average performance does not eliminate safety or reliability concerns.

# Stage 9 — Accountability, Contestability & Redress

38. [Responsibility]
39. [Accountability]
40. [Contestability]
41. [Redress]

### Key Question

> Who is answerable when an AI-supported decision causes harm, and can affected people challenge, correct, or seek remedy for that decision?

### Core Insight

An AI system should not create a responsibility vacuum.

Ethical governance requires mechanisms through which:

- responsibilities can be identified
- decisions can be reviewed
- affected people can challenge outcomes
- errors can be corrected
- harms can be remedied

Accountability is therefore more than simply identifying who developed the model.

# Stage 10 — Governance & Legitimacy

42. [AI Governance]
43. [Legitimacy]
44. [Ethics vs Law]

### Key Question

> Who has legitimate authority to approve, restrict, audit, govern, or stop an AI system?

### Core Insight

Responsible AI practice depends not only on individual developers but also on organizational and institutional structures.

Governance may involve:

- decision rights
- internal policies
- oversight mechanisms
- regulation
- standards
- audits
- approval processes
- accountability structures

Important:

> **Legal compliance ≠ Overall ethical acceptability**

Compliance may be required, but ethical evaluation can raise questions that existing regulation does not fully resolve.

# Stage 11 — Misuse & Information Integrity

45. [015 MOC - Misuse Manipulation & Information Integrity]

### Key Question

> How could the capabilities of an AI system be deliberately misused, exploited, or used to manipulate people or information environments?

### Core Insight

Ethical evaluation should consider not only intended use but also reasonably foreseeable misuse.

Relevant concerns may include:

- deception
- manipulation
- fraud
- malicious automation
- misinformation
- synthetic media
- abuse of system capabilities

A beneficial intended purpose does not eliminate misuse risk.

# Stage 12 — Sustainability & Lifecycle Responsibility

46. [016 MOC - Sustainability & Environmental Impact]
47. [Post-Deployment Monitoring]

### Key Question

> What ethical concerns may emerge over the full lifecycle of an AI system, including after deployment?

### Core Insight

Ethical evaluation does not end when a system is deployed.

Relevant concerns include:

- energy and resource use
- environmental impacts
- infrastructure requirements
- changing populations
- changing data
- model updates
- new use contexts
- emerging harms
- performance or fairness drift

Therefore:

> **Initial acceptability ≠ Permanent acceptability**

Systems may require continued monitoring, reassessment, restriction, redesign, or retirement.

# Stage 13 — Ethical Trade-offs & Decision Reasoning

48. [Ethical Trade-offs](<Ethical Trade-offs>)

Explore potential tensions such as:

- [Fairness vs Performance]
- [Privacy vs Fairness Auditing]
- [Accuracy vs Explainability]

### Key Question

> How should we reason when legitimate ethical objectives appear to conflict, and how can we determine whether the trade-off is genuine?

### Core Insight

Ethical evaluation is not about maximizing one desirable property.

It requires reasoned judgment across:

- values
- rights
- harms
- stakeholder interests
- evidence
- uncertainty
- available alternatives

Potential trade-offs should not automatically be assumed to be unavoidable.

> **A trade-off should be demonstrated rather than presumed.**

Some apparent conflicts can be reduced through:

- better problem formulation
- better data
- redesign
- different controls
- alternative workflows
- non-AI alternatives

## Evidence & Uncertainty — A Beginner Principle

Throughout every stage, distinguish between:

**What is observed**

↓

**What may explain it**

↓

**What ethical principle is relevant**

↓

**What ought to be done**

For example:

> Group A has a higher false-positive rate than Group B.

is an empirical observation.

It does not automatically establish:

> The system is discriminatory.

Further analysis may require evidence about:

- measurement
- historical conditions
- affected groups
- causal mechanisms
- resulting harms
- relevant rights
- statistical uncertainty
- decision context

Therefore:

> **Empirical evidence informs ethical judgment, but it does not replace normative reasoning.**

# Final Reflection

After completing the beginner path, select a real or hypothetical AI system and ask:

1. **Purpose** — What is the system intended to do?
2. **Alternatives** — Should AI be used for this task, or is there a less intrusive or less risky alternative?
3. **Stakeholders** — Who benefits, and who is affected?
4. **Power** — Who has decision-making power, and who may have little ability to refuse or challenge the system?
5. **Harm** — Who may be harmed, and how?
6. **Rights & Justice** — Which rights, freedoms, opportunities, or forms of disadvantage may be affected?
7. **Data** — What data are used, how were they generated, and who may be missing?
8. **Bias** — Where can systematic distortion or disadvantage enter?
9. **Fairness** — What conception of fairness is ethically relevant in this context?
10. **Transparency** — What should different stakeholders be able to know or understand?
11. **Human Agency** — Where must humans retain meaningful authority or control?
12. **Privacy** — What personal or sensitive information is collected, inferred, retained, or shared?
13. **Safety & Security** — How can the system fail, degrade, or be manipulated?
14. **Accountability** — Who is answerable for the system and its consequences?
15. **Contestability & Redress** — Can affected people challenge a decision and obtain correction or remedy?
16. **Governance & Legitimacy** — Who has authority to approve, restrict, audit, or stop the system?
17. **Misuse** — How could the system be intentionally misused or exploited?
18. **Sustainability** — What environmental or resource impacts arise across its lifecycle?
19. **Evidence & Uncertainty** — What evidence supports the relevant claims, and what remains uncertain?
20. **Monitoring** — How will risks, impacts, and system behavior be monitored after deployment?
21. **Trade-offs** — Which ethical objectives appear to conflict, and is the conflict genuinely unavoidable?
22. **Intervention** — What evidence would justify restriction, redesign, suspension, or retirement?

If these questions can be asked clearly and their answers critically examined, the beginner learning objective has largely been achieved.
